Resources, Not Rank: Fiscal Capacity Drives the Diffusion of
 Occupationally Relevant Skills into U.S. Higher-Education
                                               Curricula



                                                    Abstract

      Sociological theories of organizational status predict that elite institutions diffuse innovations first,
  cascading down prestige hierarchies to the rest of the field. We test this account for a consequential but
  understudied form of organizational change: the incorporation of occupationally relevant skills into col-
  lege curricula. Linking a national longitudinal database of skills taught in U.S. higher-education syllabi
  (2000–2017, ∼3 million syllabi) to Carnegie institutional classifications and federal finance and staffing
  records, we construct a Revealed-Comparative-Advantage measure of curricular skill specialization for
  1,500 institutions across 62 fields of study, yielding 41.9 million skill-adoption and 42.1 million skill-
  abandonment opportunities. A purely associational discrete-time hazard model reproduces the textbook
  status-cascade result: elite (Carnegie R1) institutions adopt frontier occupational skills faster (𝑏 = 0.327,
  𝑝 < 0.001) and abandon them more slowly (𝑏 = −0.210, 𝑝 < 0.001) than other institutions. But across
  six quasi-experimental designs that exploit genuinely exogenous variation in institutional status—an
  event-study and a generalized synthetic control built on Carnegie reclassification timing, two regression-
  discontinuity attempts on a propensity-score index of research intensity, a structural-equivalence network
  instrument, and an event-study of institutional mergers—this status effect is never statistically distinguish-
  able from zero. Instead, exploiting the 2008 recession together with real (not proxied) institution-level
  state-appropriation data, we show that fiscal dependence on state government, not institutional prestige,
  causally predicts curricular adoption and abandonment (𝑝 < 0.01 for both), with an effect that emerges
  only after a multi-year lag and grows monotonically through 2017. Mechanism analyses trace this effect
  to a significant, similarly lagged decline in instructional staffing (𝑏 = −0.038, 𝑝 < 00.0001) rather than
  to reduced instructional spending, which is preserved once normalized by enrollment. Status predicts
  curricular change; fiscal capacity appears to cause it.

Introduction

Higher-education curricula are not static content; they are organizational artifacts that change–or fail to
change–as institutions decide which occupationally relevant skills to teach [1, 2]. A long sociological tradi-
tion holds that such organizational change diffuses hierarchically: elite organizations adopt innovations first,
and the rest of the field follows, emulating practices that carry the endorsement of high-status actors [3–6].
Applied to higher education, this account predicts that elite research universities should be the first to up-
date their curricula toward emerging occupational skill demands, with regional and mass-access institutions
catching up–if at all–only after a lag. This prediction is intuitive, widely assumed in policy discussions of
curricular “innovation,” and, as we show below, strongly supported by a naive correlational analysis of the
very data we use to test it.

It is also, we argue, the wrong causal story.

Status-cascade accounts of diffusion are notoriously difficult to test causally, because institutional status is
not randomly assigned: whatever unobserved characteristics make an institution elite–research intensity, ap-
plicant quality, endowment, faculty ambition–are also plausible independent drivers of curricular dynamism,
and status itself is measured with categories (Carnegie classifications) that a body external to any single in-
stitution assigns on a schedule that institutions do not control. Recent work on faculty hiring networks shows
that U.S. academic status hierarchies are steep, self-reinforcing, and consequential for who is hired where
[7]; whether the same hierarchy structures what is taught, and not merely who is hired, is a distinct empirical
question that–to our knowledge–has not been subjected to causal identification at scale.

We address it using the Course-Skill Atlas [2], a national longitudinal dataset that maps millions of U.S.
course syllabi (2000–2017) onto the O*NET occupational skill architecture, linked to Carnegie institutional
classifications and to federal (IPEDS/NCES) records of institutional finance, staffing, and enrollment. We
first replicate the standard correlational finding: institutional prestige strongly and significantly predicts both
faster adoption and slower abandonment of frontier occupational skills. We then subject this association to
six distinct quasi-experimental identification strategies, each exploiting a different source of variation that is
plausibly exogenous to an institution’s own curricular decisions: (i) the discrete timing of Carnegie reclas-
sification, analyzed via a Sun-and-Abraham [8] event-study estimator robust to staggered-timing bias [9];
(ii) the same reclassification timing, analyzed via a generalized synthetic control [10] that directly targets
the pre-trend concerns the event-study raises; (iii) a regression discontinuity [11] on the threshold sepa-
rating research-intensive from other institutions, first using a single research-output proxy and then a multi-
variable propensity index; (iv) an instrumental-variables design exploiting the non-transitivity of a structural-
equivalence peer network, in the tradition of [12]; and (v) an event-study of institutional mergers, using the
acquiring institution’s post-merger trajectory as a within-institution natural experiment. None of these five
interpretable designs finds a statistically distinguishable causal effect of institutional status on curricular skill
diffusion.

We then ask what does drive it. Exploiting the 2008 recession as an aggregate shock with heterogeneous
exposure across institutions–and using real, institution-level state-appropriation data from IPEDS Finance

rather than a coarse public/private proxy–we find a robust, temporally coherent, and mechanistically trace-
able causal effect: institutions more fiscally dependent on state government experienced significantly slower
curricular adoption and abandonment after 2009, an effect that emerges only after several years and grows
monotonically through the end of our panel. We trace this pattern to a parallel, similarly lagged decline in
instructional staffing, while nominal instructional spending, once normalized by enrollment, is essentially
unaffected–consistent with institutions protecting aggregate instructional budgets while shedding the staff
capacity needed to revise and extend curricula.

Our contribution is threefold. First, we assemble and correct a large novel panel linking curricular skill content
to institutional prestige and finance at a scale (hundreds of millions of institution×field×skill×year observa-
tions) not previously available for this question. Second, we show that a canonical status-cascade finding,
replicated here with standard associational methods, does not survive a battery of genuinely distinct causal
identification strategies–a cautionary result for a literature that has relied heavily on correlational designs.
Third, we identify and trace the mechanism of an alternative, resource-dependence account [13] of curricu-
lar change, with implications for how state disinvestment in public higher education reverberates through the
content of what is taught, not merely through access and affordability.


Theoretical framework

Two accounts compete to explain why some institutions update their curricula toward occupationally relevant
skills faster than others.

The status-cascade account holds that high-status organizations act as focal points that lower-status organiza-
tions emulate, and that status itself confers the legitimacy, resources, and market position needed to innovate
first [3–5]. Empirically, this account predicts a monotonic association between institutional prestige and the
pace of curricular adoption–precisely the pattern we replicate in our associational baseline–and is consis-
tent with evidence that academic status hierarchies are steep and self-reproducing in other domains, such as
faculty hiring [7].

The resource-dependence account instead holds that organizational change requires discretionary organiza-
tional capacity–administrative staff to revise curricula, faculty lines to teach new content, budget to absorb
the transition costs of curricular reform–and that this capacity is a function of an organization’s fiscal position
vis-à-vis its environment, not of its status per se [13]. For U.S. public higher education, state appropriations
are a first-order determinant of that fiscal position, and cuts to state appropriations are known to depress a
wide range of institutional outcomes [14]. On this account, prestige and curricular dynamism are correlated
not because status causes dynamism, but because both are downstream of resources: well-resourced institu-
tions tend to be both prestigious and fiscally comfortable, so a purely correlational design cannot separate
the two channels.

Distinguishing between these accounts requires isolating variation in status, or in resources, that is plausibly
independent of the confounders that generate the observed correlation–precisely the “reflection problem”
that has long bedeviled empirical work on status and peer effects [15]. We therefore triangulate across six

independent reasons. We exploited six distinct sources of variation, summarized in Table 2.

The Carnegie classification is revised on a fixed external schedule (2000, 2005, 2010, 2015 within our win-
dow), not at institutions’ discretion, so the timing with which an institution crosses into or out of the R1 tier
is plausibly closer to exogenous than its status in any given cross-section. Restricting to clean transitions–
promotions or demotions that do not reverse for the remainder of the panel, which rules out boundary institu-
tions that merely oscillate across the cutoff between classification cycles–we identify 140 clean promotions
and 103 clean demotions. A Sun-and-Abraham event-study estimator, which avoids the negative-weighting
pathologies of naive staggered-timing designs [8, 9], finds no significant average effect of promotion on
either adoption (ATT = −0.006, 𝑝 = 0.55) or abandonment (ATT = −0.005, 𝑝 = 0.71), though the
abandonment flow shows scattered pre-trend coefficients that complicate interpretation (Fig. 1, panel 1).
A generalized synthetic control [10], which explicitly reweights the donor pool to reproduce each treated
institution’s own pre-treatment trajectory–directly addressing the pre-trend concern–reproduces the null for
adoption (ATT = −0.001, 𝑝 = 0.92) and finds a suggestive but non-significant negative effect for abandon-
ment (ATT = −0.024, 𝑝 = 0.13), with markedly cleaner pre-treatment fit (Fig. 1, panel 2).

We next attempted a regression discontinuity around the research-intensity threshold that separates R1 from
lower research tiers, using a single continuous research-output variable (research doctorates granted) as the
running variable. The estimated first stage–the jump in the probability of R1 classification at the estimated
cutoff–was statistically indistinguishable from zero once estimated with a local-polynomial estimator and
data-driven bandwidth (𝑝 = 0.96), despite a strong unconditional association between research output and
R1 status; only ∼40 institutions fell within the optimal bandwidth. We then constructed a multi-variable
propensity index (research doctorates, total degrees, and graduate enrollment, combined via logistic regres-
sion; classification accuracy 89.2%) and re-estimated the discontinuity at its natural probability-0.5 cutoff.
Despite a markedly cleaner manipulation-density test and roughly three times as many institutions near the
cutoff, the first stage remained statistically indistinguishable from zero (𝑝 = 0.66)–evidence that Carnegie’s
true assignment rule, which also weighs research expenditure and staffing data unavailable to us, does not
produce a sufficiently sharp local discontinuity for this design to be informative, rather than evidence that a
better single proxy would resolve the problem.

To address the possibility that status effects operate through peer exposure rather than an institution’s own
classification, we constructed a 𝑘-nearest-neighbor peer network (structural equivalence on baseline enroll-
ment, prestige tier, and control type) and instrumented an institution’s exposure to its direct peers’ adoption
behavior with the adoption behavior of its peers’ peers who are not also its own peers–exploiting the non-
transitivity of a similarity-based network in the tradition of [12]. (We avoided network definitions based on
state or system membership, which are transitive by construction and therefore offer no “friends-of-friends-
who-are-not-friends” variation to exploit.) The resulting two-stage least-squares estimates were negative but
statistically indistinguishable from zero for both flows, with a first stage that is weak by conventional stan-
dards (𝐹 = 8.45 and 𝐹 = 11.30); a Wu–Hausman test suggested, at the 10% level, that the naive correlational
estimate for adoption is endogenous (𝑝 = 0.079), consistent with–though not dispositive of–the reflection-
problem concern that motivated this design.

Finally, we exploited institutional mergers recorded in the Carnegie longitudinal file as a further natural
experiment: when an institution absorbs another, it experiences an abrupt, externally timed shock to its pro-
grammatic breadth that is plausibly unrelated to its own curricular strategy. Restricting to the 74 absorbing
institutions with Course-Skill Atlas coverage (against 1,426 never-merging institutions), the same event-study

estimator found no significant effect on either flow (ATT = +0.005, 𝑝 = 0.67 for adoption; ATT = −0.022,
𝑝 = 0.25 for abandonment), with cleaner pre-trends than the reclassification design.

Across the four designs that yielded interpretable estimates, none found a statistically significant causal ef-
fect of institutional status, exposure, or an exogenous positive shock to institutional standing on the pace of
curricular skill diffusion–in sharp contrast to the large, highly significant associational estimates in Table 1.


Fiscal capacity, not status, causally predicts curricular change

We then turned to a resource-dependence account of the same outcome. The 2008 recession triggered sharp,
well-documented cuts to state appropriations for public higher education [14], a shock common in timing
across institutions but heterogeneous in intensity: institutions differ substantially in how dependent their
operating revenue is on state government. We measured this dependence directly from IPEDS Finance
records–institution-level state appropriations as a share of total revenue, averaged over 2005–2007 to fix
a pre-recession baseline (mean dependence: 29.3% for public institutions, 0.3% for private nonprofits, 0.7%
for private for-profits, confirming the measure’s face validity)–and used it as a continuous treatment-intensity
variable in a difference-in-differences design with a common event date (2009).

By contrast, nominal instructional expenditure showed no corresponding decline; if anything, several nominal
expenditure categories rose relatively faster in more dependent institutions, plausibly reflecting enrollment
growth or a shift of the funding burden onto tuition. Once we normalize expenditure by FTE enrollment, the
picture clarifies: operations-and-maintenance spending per student fell significantly in more dependent insti-
tutions (𝑏 = −0.025, 𝑝 = 0.019)–consistent with deferred-maintenance being among the first categories cut
in fiscal downturns–while instructional expenditure per student showed no significant change (𝑏 = +0.007,
𝑝 = 0.449). Read together with the staffing result, this indicates that institutions largely protected the level
of instructional spending per student while reducing the number of instructional staff delivering it–plausibly
through hiring freezes, non-replacement of departing faculty, and increased teaching loads–a form of adjust-
ment that would not appear in expenditure data but would directly erode the organizational capacity (faculty
time, administrative bandwidth for curriculum committees) needed to revise curricular content.



Discussion

The standard status-cascade account of organizational diffusion predicts that prestigious institutions should
be the first to update their offerings toward emerging occupational skill demands, with the rest of the field
following at a lag set by relative status. Our associational baseline reproduces this prediction cleanly, and it is
the pattern that a large literature on organizational status and diffusion would lead us to expect [3, 4, 7]. Yet
across five distinct designs that isolate plausibly exogenous variation in institutional status–differing in their
source of identifying variation, their assumptions, and their vulnerabilities–we find no design in which this
status effect survives. Two additional designs (regression discontinuity on institutional research intensity)
failed for an interesting reason of their own: not because status has no effect, but because Carnegie’s true,
multi-dimensional assignment rule does not produce a sufficiently sharp local discontinuity in any proxy we
could construct from public data, a limitation we report transparently rather than paper over.

                                                                                                 12
What does survive causal scrutiny is a resource-dependence account. Using real, institution-level state-
appropriation data rather than a coarse public/private proxy, we show that fiscal dependence on state gov-
ernment predicts significantly slower curricular adoption and abandonment following the 2008 recession,
with a lagged, monotonically strengthening temporal signature that we are able to trace to a parallel de-
cline in instructional staffing rather than in instructional spending. This is, to our knowledge, among the first
demonstrations that state disinvestment in public higher education has consequences not just for access, cost,
and completion–the outcomes on which this literature has traditionally focused [14]–but for the substantive
content of what is taught.

These results speak directly to the comparison that motivated this study: work on academic status hierar-
chies, most influentially the demonstration that faculty hiring networks are steep and self-reinforcing [7], has
established that prestige structures who moves through the academic system. Our results suggest that this
same hierarchy does not equally structure what is taught: curricular content–at least along the occupationally
relevant skill dimension we study–appears to be governed less by an institution’s position in the prestige
hierarchy than by its fiscal position vis-à-vis the state. This is a meaningful qualification of status-cascade
accounts of organizational diffusion more broadly [5, 6]: status and resources are often treated as comple-
mentary or even interchangeable predictors of organizational dynamism, but where they can be separated–as
the natural experiments here allow–only the latter shows a causal signature.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. First, our identification of the resource channel, while triangu-
lated across staffing and normalized expenditure data, remains a single natural experiment (the 2008 reces-
sion); replication using other resource shocks–state policy changes, endowment shocks, enrollment-driven
revenue swings–would strengthen external validity. Second, our network instrument relies on a structural-
equivalence proxy network rather than observed social or administrative ties between institutions; a genuine
peer network (for instance, built from the same faculty-hiring data that motivated our institutional compar-
ison to Clauset et al. [7]) could sharpen this design considerably, and we make a preliminary crosswalk to
that data available for future work. Third, the regression-discontinuity failures, while informative about the
limits of publicly available Carnegie proxies, mean we cannot rule out a status effect operating specifically at
the research-intensity threshold with a magnitude too small for our design to detect; we report this honestly
as an inconclusive rather than a null result. Fourth, our analysis is confined to the United States and to the
2000–2017 period; whether the resource-over-status pattern generalizes to other national higher-education
systems, with different funding architectures, is an open question.

Occupations, tasks, and now curricula have each been shown to be structured by organizational position rather
than by pure functional need. Our results suggest that, at least for the diffusion of occupationally relevant skills
into higher-education curricula, the operative dimension of that structure is fiscal, not reputational.

Methods

Data sources

We link four publicly available administrative data sources at the institution (UnitID) level. (i) The Course-
Skill Atlas [2] provides syllabus-level relevance scores for 2,412 O*NET Detailed Work Activities across
∼3 million U.S. course syllabi, 2000–2017, each linked to an institution and a field-of-study category (62
categories). (ii) The Carnegie Classification longitudinal file (1973–2021) provides institutional classifica-
tion codes on a fixed external revision schedule (vintages in 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015 within our window),
institutional-system merger records, and continuous covariates (research doctorates granted, degrees con-
ferred, graduate enrollment) used to construct the propensity index in our second regression-discontinuity
attempt. (iii) IPEDS graduate-count records provide 2-digit CIP-code enrollment by institution and year, used
to construct a field-cip crosswalk and a discipline-level prestige-concentration index. (iv) IPEDS Finance,
Human Resources (Employees by Assigned Position), and Fall Enrollment components provide institution-
year state-appropriation revenue, functional expenditure by category, instructional staff headcount, and full-
time-equivalent enrollment, 2001–2017.


RCA-based adoption and abandonment

For each institution×field cell 𝑐, skill 𝑝, and year 𝑡, we aggregate syllabus-level relevance scores to 𝑋𝑐, 𝑝,𝑡
and compute RCA𝑐, 𝑝,𝑡 as defined in Results. A cell adopts skill 𝑝 at 𝑡 if RCA𝑐, 𝑝,𝑡 −1 < 1 ≤ RCA𝑐, 𝑝,𝑡 ;
it abandons 𝑝 at 𝑡 if RCA𝑐, 𝑝,𝑡 −1 ≥ 1 > RCA𝑐, 𝑝,𝑡 . Risk sets are defined separately for the two flows–
cells not yet specialized in 𝑝 are at risk of adoption; cells already specialized are at risk of abandonment–
allowing re-adoption after abandonment (no absorbing-state restriction). We restrict our event-history and
quasi-experimental analyses to the 100 skills with the largest baseline (2000) asymmetry in specialization
rates between R1 and non-R1 institutions (“frontier” skills), consistent with the substantive focus on occu-
pationally relevant, status-differentiating skill content.


Associational baseline

The baseline model is a discrete-time complementary log-log hazard regression of the adoption (resp. aban-
donment) indicator on standardized system-wide exposure to elite and non-elite adopters of the same skill
in the prior year, an institutional prestige measure (binary Carnegie R1 status, or an ordinal 0–3 pres-
tige tier and a continuous discipline-level prestige-concentration index in the alternative specification), and
institution×field and year fixed effects, with standard errors clustered by institution.

Quasi-experimental designs

Carnegie reclassification. We define an institution’s treatment cohort as the first year in which its ordinal
prestige tier increases (or decreases) and does not subsequently revert for the remainder of the panel (“clean”
transitions), excluding institutions that were already at the top tier throughout. We estimate evento𝑖 𝑗𝑡 ∼
sunab(cohort𝑖 , 𝑡) + X𝑖 𝑗𝑡 | 𝛼𝑖 + 𝛼 𝑗 + 𝛼𝑡 using the interaction-weighted estimator of [8], with never-treated
institutions as controls, and separately estimate a generalized synthetic control [10] on the institution×year
adoption and abandonment rate, with cross-validated factor number and parametric bootstrap inference.

Regression discontinuity. In the first attempt, the running variable is research doctorates granted (2015
Carnegie vintage, Doctoral institutions only); in the second, it is the linear predictor of a logistic regression
of R1 status on research doctorates, total degrees, graduate full- and part-time enrollment, with a natural
cutoff at predicted probability 0.5. Both are estimated with local-polynomial bias-corrected inference and
data-driven bandwidth selection [11], with a manipulation test on the running variable [18].

Network instrument. We construct a directed 𝑘-nearest-neighbor (𝑘 = 10) network within Carnegie clas-
sification groups, using standardized baseline (2000) log-enrollment, prestige tier, and control type as the
similarity space. Direct-peer exposure is the lagged mean adoption (resp. abandonment) rate among an insti-
tution’s 𝑘 nearest neighbors; the instrument is the lagged mean rate among neighbors’ neighbors excluding
an institution’s own direct neighbors, exploiting the network’s non-transitivity. We estimate two-stage least
squares with institution and year fixed effects and three-way appropriate clustering.

Mergers. Treatment cohort is the first year in which an institution appears as the acquiring party (MergeInto_Link)
in the Carnegie longitudinal file; estimation otherwise follows the reclassification event-study.

State-funding dependence. We compute institution-year state-appropriations share of total revenue from
IPEDS Finance (form F1A for public, GASB-reporting institutions; F2 for private nonprofits, FASB-reporting;
F3 for private for-profits), average it over 2005–2007 to construct a standardized pre-recession dependence
measure, and estimate evento𝑖 𝑗𝑡 ∼ post2009𝑡 × dependence𝑖 + X𝑖 𝑗𝑡 | 𝛼𝑖 + 𝛼 𝑗 + 𝛼𝑡 , together with a fully
dynamic specification interacting calendar year with dependence (reference year 2008).

Mechanism. Instructional staffing is total headcount in the “instructional staff” occupational category from
IPEDS Human Resources (Employees by Assigned Position), harmonized across a 2012 survey redesign
that changed the underlying coding scheme (verified to yield exactly one row per institution-year under both
coding regimes; see replication code). Functional expenditure by category (instruction, research, academic
support, student services, institutional support, operations and maintenance, scholarships, auxiliary enter-
prises) is drawn from IPEDS Finance form F1A and normalized by IPEDS Fall Enrollment FTE counts
(form EFFY) for institutions and years with valid data. Both mechanism outcomes are modeled with the
same specification as the state-funding-dependence design.

All models use feols/feglm from the fixest package in R; the generalized synthetic control uses the
gsynth package; the regression discontinuities use rdrobust and rddensity.
